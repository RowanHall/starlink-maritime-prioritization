Starlink Performance on a Moving Vehicle

1. Problem

Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellite networks like Starlink offer broadband connectivity almost anywhere, but their real-world performance changes quite a bit once the terminal starts moving. Mobile terminals are now found mounted on buses, service vehicles, boats, and RVs, where users expect stable internet for navigation, telemetry, or remote work. However, vehicular deployments frequently suffer from sudden latency spikes, poor throughput, and brief connection dropouts.

The underlying causes of these mobile performance drops are not yet fully understood or explored. When a vehicle is in motion, network reliability depends on a mix of overhead obstacles (such as tree canopies, terrain cuts, and overpasses) and rapid orbital dynamics, where terminals must switch beams or satellites every few seconds. Because existing studies look at isolated drives, it is difficult to determine whether a bad connection is an unavoidable spot on the road, an inefficient satellite handover, or bad weather. Understanding where and why these dropouts occur is essential for setting realistic Quality of Service (QoS) expectations, planning routes with reliable connection, and designing smarter handover protocols for mobile satellite systems.

2. Prior Work

Laniewski et al. [1] conducted a comprehensive evaluation of mobile Starlink performance using a Flat High Performance (FHP) dish mounted on a service van in Germany over a two-month period. Capturing over 15,000 stationary and mobile samples, they observed a mobile throughput degradation of ~10%. Unfortunately, their dataset remains limited by a relatively short observation window.

Laniewski et al. [2] conducted a 300 km Autobahn test drive at fixed speeds up to 140 km/h, measuring signal degradation as the vehicle passed beneath 98 highway overpasses. Their study demonstrated significant performance degradation under high speed: mobile packet loss increased by 814% and median RTT increased by 45% relative to stationary controls. While their tests highlight short-term high-speed conditions with obstructions, the study is constrained by its brief timeframe.

Zhao et al. [3] investigated Starlink handovers under vehicular mobility. By analyzing telemetry from a 5-hour drive, they found that mobile terminals frequently break the standard 15-second handover interval to circumvent transient physical obstructions. These dynamic beam switching events cause severe network interruptions taking up to two seconds to recover. However, their short observation window cannot reveal long-term geographic or seasonal performance trends.

3. Approach

Most mobile Starlink studies rely on a single trip or a few weeks of data. In contrast, our approach takes advantage of a massive 1.5-year dataset collected from a school bus running the exact same two-hour route, twice a day, every weekday.

Because the bus drives the same road hundreds of times across different seasons, weather, traffic conditions, and use conditions, it acts as a natural controlled experiment. Holding the route constant makes it much easier to figure out what actually causes bad performance, indicating whether a drop in service is tied to a specific spot on the road (like trees, hills, or bridges) or to satellite issues overhead.

Our approach focuses on three main steps:

  • Mapping Route Hotspots: We map latency and packet loss along the entire route to see if specific spots consistently suffer from poor connectivity, and whether those trouble spots stay the same from day to day.
  • Comparing Trips Over Time: We compare morning versus afternoon runs, weekdays versus weekends, and changes across seasons to see how time of day, network traffic, and weather affect performance.
  • Tracking Satellite Handovers: Using logged satellite IDs and GPS, we track every time the terminal switches satellites to see how often handovers trigger latency spikes or temporary dropouts, and whether those handovers are scheduled or forced by blockages.
