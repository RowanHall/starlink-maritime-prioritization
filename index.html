Prioritizing Maritime Starlink Traffic for Commercial Fishing Vessels

Edge traffic prioritization and Active Queue Management to protect latency-sensitive operational traffic under heavy crew demand on fluctuating satellite links.

Jasper McLennan · Rowan Hall (Open to a third member — reach out in Teams.)

Problem Prior Work Approach Deliverables Schedule Resources References

1. Problem & Motivation

Commercial fishing vessels increasingly rely on Starlink for both essential operations and crew welfare. A recurring operational challenge is severe uplink saturation and bufferbloat: when off-shift crew use high-bandwidth applications (video streaming, cloud backups, large downloads), the shared link's queues fill and latency spikes from tens of milliseconds into seconds. These spikes degrade and occasionally disrupt latency-sensitive operational traffic — bridge communications, vessel telematics, position reporting, and messaging.

At sea there is no fallback link. The practical response today is a blunt one: operators enforce strict rules that limit crew to messaging and occasional browsing to avoid interfering with ship operations. This protects the bridge but wastes capacity that is often idle, and it treats a scheduling problem as a policy problem. Having worked in the fishing industry, we have lived this trade-off directly. The question we want to answer is whether smart queue management at the edge can protect critical traffic automatically, letting crew use the network freely without ever taking down bridge communications.

2. What Has Been Done — and Why It Falls Short

The networking community has largely solved bufferbloat on stable links. Active Queue Management (AQM) algorithms such as CoDel and its flow-queuing variant FQ-CoDel keep standing queues short by targeting queue sojourn time rather than occupancy, and CAKE bundles shaping, flow isolation, and host fairness into a single tin. These are mature, deployed, and well understood — but they assume a reasonably steady egress rate.

Starlink breaks that assumption. Its low-Earth-orbit capacity fluctuates on sub-second to multi-second timescales as satellites hand off, with periodic reconfiguration intervals and weather- and obstruction-driven variability. A fixed shaper rate set conservatively wastes capacity; set optimistically, it reintroduces the very queues AQM is meant to drain. Vendor-side QoS on the terminal is opaque and not tunable per vessel, and consumer prioritization features do not express maritime operational intent (e.g. "bridge telematics always wins, crew streaming yields"). Published Starlink measurement studies document the latency variability but stop short of evaluating edge QoS policies tuned to it. Prior AQM work rarely targets a link this volatile or this operationally critical.

3. Our Approach — Why We Can Do Better

We will place an edge gateway between the Starlink terminal and the vessel LAN and treat prioritization as an adaptive control problem rather than a static configuration. Two complementary techniques:

  • Operational-intent QoS: classify traffic into tiers (operational / interactive / bulk) and give operational traffic strict priority with flow-isolated AQM (FQ-CoDel or CAKE's diffserv modes) so crew bulk transfers can only consume the capacity that operational traffic is not using.
  • Dynamic rate adaptation: because a fixed shaper rate is wrong on Starlink, we will track the link's recent achievable rate and latency and adjust the shaper continuously — keeping the bottleneck queue at the edge, where we control it, instead of inside the opaque terminal.

Our differentiator is the setting and the evaluation: we tune and measure these policies against Starlink's real volatility and against a concrete maritime objective — bounded worst-case latency for operational traffic under adversarial crew load — rather than average throughput on a stable testbed. Our firsthand knowledge of vessel operations lets us define realistic traffic classes and load scenarios.

Concretely, the work proceeds in two phases:

  1. Benchmark & test locally. Establish baseline latency-under-load, then compare queueing/QoS policies on a controlled testbed (and, if available, UVic's Starlink setup). Standard bufferbloat tooling (flent, RRUL, irtt) drives reproducible load.
  2. Deploy on a real vessel terminal. Run the most promising configuration on an edge router connected to an active Starlink terminal and measure real-world improvement under genuine crew load.

4. Expected Deliverables

  • A reproducible benchmark methodology and baseline dataset characterizing latency-under-load on Starlink.
  • A comparative evaluation of AQM/QoS policies (FQ-CoDel, CAKE, static vs. dynamic rate shaping) against latency and fairness metrics.
  • A documented edge-gateway configuration (scripts / router config) implementing the best policy.
  • Real-world results from deployment on an active vessel terminal, where access permits.
  • A final report and this public project website, kept current throughout the term.

  • Starlink access for testing — ideally UVic's Starlink setup for controlled experiments, plus an active vessel terminal for field deployment. (Pending confirmation of UVic Starlink availability for student experiments.)
